SaaS User Provisioning and Deprovisioning: A Joiner, Mover and Leaver Checklist

SaaS user provisioning is the process of giving a person the application accounts and permissions required for their role. Deprovisioning removes access when it is no longer needed. A repeatable lifecycle helps teams avoid delayed onboarding, excessive permissions and accounts that remain active after a person leaves.

SaaS user provisioning and deprovisioning should be treated as one controlled lifecycle, not as isolated account-creation tickets. A person may join, change roles, work with a temporary project team or leave the organisation. Each event can change which applications and data they should be allowed to access.

What provisioning and deprovisioning cover

Provisioning creates or enables an account, assigns the correct licence, applies a role and provides approved access. Deprovisioning removes or disables access when a person no longer has a valid business need. Both processes should be traceable to an authorised request or a reliable employment and contract record.

For many organisations, the lifecycle has three familiar stages: joiner, mover and leaver. A joiner receives the access needed for a new role. A mover has existing permissions reviewed when responsibilities change. A leaver has access removed in line with the organisation's offboarding process.

Build a single access inventory

Start with a current list of business applications, owners, authentication methods, user groups and sensitive data. Include cloud consoles, collaboration tools, finance platforms, customer systems, code repositories and specialist services. An application inventory helps HR, IT and business owners understand where an account must be created, changed or removed.

Record the minimum useful information: application name, business owner, data sensitivity, access method, account type, approval owner, provisioning method and offboarding route. Avoid storing passwords, API keys or recovery codes in a spreadsheet. Keep secrets in an approved secrets manager or identity platform.

Joiner workflow: grant only approved access

  1. Confirm the person and start date. Use the authoritative HR or contractor record, not an informal message alone.
  2. Define the role. Map the role to an approved access profile and document any exceptions.
  3. Obtain approvals. Ask the relevant manager and application owner to approve privileged or sensitive access.
  4. Create the identity. Apply the organisation's authentication, MFA and recovery requirements before enabling important services.
  5. Assign licences and groups. Use role-based groups where practical instead of manually granting broad permissions.
  6. Verify first-day access. Confirm the employee can reach required systems and that unneeded applications were not assigned.

Keep standard roles small and review exceptions. A new team member may need a customer-support system but not a billing administrator role. A temporary access grant should have an owner, an expiry or review date and a clear business reason.

Mover workflow: review access when responsibilities change

Role changes can create permission accumulation. A person moving from sales to finance may need a different set of applications, while older access should be removed. Do not simply add the new role's groups and leave every previous permission in place.

  • Confirm the effective date and the new manager or team owner.
  • Compare current access with the new role profile.
  • Remove permissions that are no longer needed.
  • Check shared mailboxes, administrator roles, API tokens and external guest access.
  • Record approval for exceptions and schedule a follow-up review.

Leaver workflow: remove access and recover business assets

Offboarding should be coordinated with the authorised owner and the organisation's HR, legal and security procedures. The timing may depend on contract terms, local law, safety needs and the person's role. The goal is to prevent unnecessary access while preserving business records and service continuity.

  1. Confirm the approved departure event and effective time.
  2. Disable sign-in or revoke sessions using the identity provider's supported controls.
  3. Remove access to SaaS applications, cloud environments, repositories and VPNs.
  4. Review privileged accounts, shared credentials, API keys, tokens and service ownership.
  5. Transfer business files, workflows and customer responsibilities to an approved owner.
  6. Recover company devices and document exceptions or dependencies.
  7. Retain or delete records according to the organisation's retention policy and applicable law.

Do not automatically delete every mailbox or user file at the moment of departure. Some information may need to be retained, transferred or preserved under a legal hold. Separate access removal from record-retention decisions and make the responsible owner explicit.

Automate carefully with SSO and SCIM

Single sign-on (SSO) lets users authenticate through a central identity provider. System for Cross-domain Identity Management (SCIM) can automate supported user and group provisioning between systems. These controls can reduce manual work, but they do not remove the need for application owners, exception handling and testing.

Before enabling automated deprovisioning, test how the target application handles suspension, deletion, ownership transfer and data retention. Some services may disable an account, while others may remove group membership or revoke licences. Confirm the vendor's behaviour and create a recovery procedure before changing production settings.

Set review intervals according to risk, regulation and the sensitivity of each system. High-impact administrator access usually deserves more frequent attention than a low-risk read-only tool. A review should end with a decision: keep, change, remove or investigate, with a named owner and recorded completion.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Creating accounts from chat messages without checking the requester's authority.
  • Leaving temporary contractor or guest accounts without a sponsor or expiry.
  • Removing a user from the identity provider but forgetting direct vendor accounts.
  • Failing to transfer ownership of dashboards, workflows, cloud resources or customer records.
  • Using shared administrator accounts that make actions difficult to attribute.
  • Assuming SCIM or SSO automatically covers every app and every account type.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between provisioning and deprovisioning?

Provisioning creates or enables an account and grants approved application access. Deprovisioning removes or disables that access when it is no longer required. A complete process also changes permissions when a person's role changes and records who approved each important decision.

What does joiner, mover and leaver mean in access management?

Joiner, mover and leaver describes the main stages of a person's relationship with an organisation. Joiners receive role-based access, movers have permissions adjusted when responsibilities change, and leavers have access removed. Each stage should have a trigger, owner, approval path and completion record.

Does single sign-on automatically remove every SaaS account?

No. Single sign-on centralises authentication for connected applications, but not every service or local account is necessarily covered. Check whether each application supports lifecycle provisioning, whether direct logins remain active and how privileged, guest and service accounts are handled during offboarding.

How often should a business review SaaS access?

Review frequency should reflect the sensitivity of the application, the privilege level and applicable requirements. Review administrator and high-risk access more frequently, and review ordinary access on a defined schedule. Also trigger a review after role changes, supplier changes, incidents or organisational restructuring.

What records should be kept when an employee leaves?

Keep evidence of the approved departure event, the time access was disabled, the applications reviewed, any tokens or devices handled, ownership transfers and unresolved exceptions. Retain business records according to legal, contractual and internal retention rules rather than deleting everything automatically.

Key takeaway

A reliable SaaS access lifecycle connects role-based provisioning, permission changes and timely offboarding. Maintain an application inventory, use approvals for sensitive access, automate only where behaviour is understood and review exceptions. Most importantly, keep evidence showing who requested, approved and completed each access change.
