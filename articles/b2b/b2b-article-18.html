B2B Statement of Work Checklist: Scope, Deliverables and Acceptance

A clear B2B statement of work turns a commercial agreement into an understandable plan for delivery. It explains what the supplier will do, what the customer must provide, how progress will be checked and what counts as accepted work. This guide covers the sections that prevent avoidable scope disputes.

Written by Zia Carter — Writer & EditorNovember 20, 202611 min read

B2B statement of work checklist planning should happen before delivery begins, not after a disagreement appears. A statement of work (SOW) records the objective, scope, deliverables, schedule, roles, assumptions, acceptance method and change process. It helps the client and supplier use the same definition of completed work.

What is a B2B statement of work?

A B2B statement of work is a project document that describes the services a supplier will perform for a business customer and the conditions for delivery. It normally sets out tasks, deliverables, milestones, timing, responsibilities, assumptions, acceptance and payment arrangements.

A proposal explains why a supplier recommends an approach and what it may cost. An RFP asks potential suppliers to respond to a buyer's requirements. A master services agreement may set general legal and commercial terms. The SOW translates the agreed project into specific work and measurable outcomes; the governing contract should explain how the documents fit together.

Define the outcome before listing tasks

Start with a short project objective that describes the business result rather than a vague activity. “Improve customer reporting” is difficult to assess. “Deliver a dashboard that lets the finance team review monthly invoice status by customer, overdue range and owner” gives both parties a clearer outcome to discuss.

Then write the scope in plain language. Identify included activities and explicit exclusions. If data migration, training, copywriting, third-party licences, new integrations or ongoing support are not included, say so. Ambiguous phrases such as “all required work” or “complete solution” can hide different expectations.

What should a statement of work include?

A useful statement of work describes the objective, included and excluded scope, tasks, tangible deliverables, schedule, roles, dependencies, acceptance rules and commercial arrangements. The exact format varies, but both sides should be able to tell what will be supplied, by whom, when and how completion will be judged.

Write deliverables so they can be checked

Describe each deliverable with an identifiable output and acceptance method. A task such as “design a website” is not a deliverable specification. A better example might be “a five-page responsive website, supplied through the agreed staging URL, with the agreed page list and a handover note.” The parties can then define browser, content and accessibility checks that suit the project.

Acceptance criteria should be observable and agreed before work starts. They may specify a file format, required fields, test cases, agreed design states, document length, delivery channel or named approver. Avoid absolute wording such as “perfect” or “works everywhere” unless the parties define exactly how it will be tested.

Set milestones and customer responsibilities

A schedule should show when the supplier plans to complete each stage and when the customer must provide inputs or approvals. Delays can arise when a customer has not supplied content, access, feedback or a decision. Record these dependencies and explain how they affect the delivery plan if they are late.

  • Name a person who can approve work and one backup contact.
  • List the data, credentials, brand assets, subject-matter feedback or environments the customer must provide.
  • Set a reasonable review period that both parties agree can be met.
  • Define what happens when a milestone is blocked by a missing dependency.

How should scope changes be controlled?

Use a written change process whenever a request alters the agreed work, cost, schedule or acceptance criteria. The supplier should describe the requested change, impact and revised estimate; the customer should approve or decline it through the agreed authority before the team treats it as part of the project.

A practical change request includes: the request date, requested outcome, reason, affected deliverables, added or removed work, fee and timing impact, risks, decision owner and approval status. Keep the original SOW and approved changes together so the current agreement is easy to identify.

Example: a small CRM implementation SOW

A fictional consultancy agrees to configure a CRM for a 12-person sales team. Its SOW lists the agreed user roles, two data-import templates, three pipeline stages, one training session and a handover guide. It excludes custom software development and historical data cleanup beyond the agreed import format.

The milestones are discovery, configuration, customer review, agreed corrections and handover. Acceptance checks confirm that the named users can sign in, the agreed fields and stages exist, the sample records import without the specified validation errors, and the training guide is delivered. If the customer later requests a second business unit or a new integration, the parties use the change process to agree its price and effect on the timeline.

Before signing: practical review checklist

  • Can a new team member understand the objective and scope without a verbal explanation?
  • Are all deliverables described as identifiable outputs?
  • Are exclusions and customer dependencies visible?
  • Do milestones include an owner and a review method?
  • Are acceptance criteria objective and achievable?
  • Are fees, expenses and payment triggers consistent with the governing agreement?
  • Is there a written method for approving changes, extensions and additional work?
  • Have privacy, information security, intellectual property and confidentiality requirements been addressed in the contract documents where relevant?

Frequently asked questions

Is a statement of work the same as a contract?

Not necessarily. A statement of work can be attached to or incorporated into a services contract, while the main agreement may contain broader legal and commercial terms. The relationship depends on the documents and governing law, so confirm precedence, signatures and required clauses before work starts.

Who should write the statement of work?

The buyer and supplier should develop it together. The buyer explains the required business outcome and constraints; the supplier explains the work, assumptions, delivery method and risks. Procurement, legal, security or finance teams may need to review sections that affect their responsibilities.

What is the difference between scope and deliverables?

Scope describes the work and boundaries of the project. Deliverables are the specific outputs produced by that work, such as a report, configured system, training session or set of files. A strong SOW connects each deliverable to tasks, timing and acceptance criteria.

Should an SOW include acceptance criteria?

Yes, where the project produces work that needs review or approval. Criteria should describe how the customer will assess each deliverable, who is authorised to accept it and how defects or rework are handled. Agree the review process before delivery to avoid subjective disputes.

How do you handle work that is not in the SOW?

Record the request and assess its effects on scope, cost, timing and risk. The supplier should submit a change description or revised estimate, and an authorised customer representative should approve it in writing before extra work begins, unless the existing agreement clearly provides another process.

Key takeaway

A strong SOW gives a B2B project a shared definition of success. Define the outcome, scope, deliverables, schedule, roles, dependencies, acceptance and change control in plain language. Then make sure the SOW aligns with the signed contract and the laws that apply to the transaction.
