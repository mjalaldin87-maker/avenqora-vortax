Online Privacy: Personal Data, Permissions and Digital Footprints

Online privacy is about understanding how information about you is collected, used, shared and stored. You cannot control every data flow, but you can make more informed choices about what you share and which permissions you grant.

What you will learn

  • Recognize direct and indirect personal information
  • Explain active and passive digital footprints
  • Review app permissions and privacy choices thoughtfully

What counts as personal data?

Personal data is information that can identify or relate to a person, either by itself or when combined with other information. Examples may include a name, email address, phone number, account identifier, location history, photographs, device identifiers or details about a person's activities.

Some information does not look identifying on its own. A combination of location, work schedule and public posts may reveal more than the person intended. Context matters.

Understand your digital footprint

Your digital footprint is the trail of information created by activities online and through connected devices. An active footprint includes information you choose to share, such as a profile post or a comment. A passive footprint can include information generated while using a service, such as device details, cookies, usage events or approximate location.

These labels describe how information is created; they do not automatically tell you whether collection is appropriate or lawful. The important questions are what is collected, why it is needed, who receives it and how long it is kept.

How websites and apps may use information

A service may use information to provide its main function, remember preferences, improve reliability, personalize content, measure usage or support advertising. Different services have different practices. Read the privacy notice and check the controls available inside the account rather than assuming that every app handles information in the same way.

Review permissions with a purpose

Before granting an app access to a camera, microphone, contacts, photos or location, ask whether the permission is needed for the feature you want to use. A navigation app may need location while directions are active. A calculator usually has no obvious reason to access your contacts.

Permission controls differ by operating system. You can review them in device settings and remove access that is no longer needed. Some services may stop working fully when a permission is denied; check the feature requirements before changing an important work or school account.

Make privacy choices in everyday use

  • Share only the details needed for the purpose of a profile or form.
  • Review the audience for posts, photos and shared documents.
  • Use separate, strong passwords and multi-factor authentication for important accounts.
  • Check connected apps and remove access you no longer recognize or use.
  • Be careful with quizzes, giveaways and forms that ask for unnecessary personal details.
  • Review location, microphone and camera access on a regular schedule.
  • Learn how the service allows you to download, correct or delete information where those controls exist.

Privacy is not the same as secrecy

Privacy choices do not make a person invisible online. A private browsing window may reduce what is saved locally on a device, but it does not make all activity invisible to websites, networks or account providers. Likewise, removing a post does not guarantee that every copy or screenshot disappears.

Worked example: a photo-sharing app

Imagine an app asks for access to your entire photo library, precise location and contacts. Before accepting, check whether the app can work with selected photos instead of the whole library, whether location tagging is optional and whether contact access is required for a feature you plan to use. Choose the smallest permission set that supports your intended use.

Practice task

  1. Write down three pieces of information you share publicly online.
  2. Identify one permission that a frequently used app requests.
  3. Explain why a location history can reveal more than a single location.
  4. Review privacy settings for one account you control and note one change you may make.

Quick review

  • Personal data can identify someone directly or in combination with other details.
  • Digital footprints include information people share and information generated by services.
  • App permissions should match the feature being used.
  • Privacy controls reduce exposure but do not guarantee complete anonymity.
