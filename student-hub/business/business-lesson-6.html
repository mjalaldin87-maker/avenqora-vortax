Business Models: How an Organization Creates and Captures Value

A business model explains how an organization creates value for customers and how it can sustain the work financially. This lesson uses an original four-question worksheet to help students analyze a simple business idea.

Prepared by Zia CarterNovember 5, 2026Beginner lesson

What you will learn

  • Explain what a business model describes
  • Connect a customer problem to an offer and delivery process
  • Use a four-question worksheet to examine costs and revenue

What is a business model?

A business model is a description of how an organization creates, delivers and captures value. It connects the customer problem, the offer, the activities and resources needed to deliver it, and the way the organization receives enough income or other support to continue.

A business model is not the same as a business plan. The model describes how the basic system works. A plan adds more detail about market research, operations, people, risks, goals and financial projections.

Start with the customer problem

A business idea should begin with a customer or user need that can be understood and tested. “Build an app” describes a product idea, not a customer problem. “Students need a reliable way to find short practice exercises before a programming exam” describes a situation that can be researched.

Ask who experiences the problem, how they solve it today, what is frustrating about the current options and whether they would value a different approach.

Explain the value being offered

The offer may be a physical product, a service, access to software, a marketplace, training or a combination. A clear value proposition explains what changes for the customer and why the offer may be more useful than the alternatives.

A strong description avoids unsupported promises. Instead of saying “the fastest service,” explain which step is made easier and how a customer can evaluate that claim.

Understand how value is delivered

Delivery requires activities and resources. A tutoring service needs suitable instructors, learning materials, scheduling, communication and a way to provide feedback. A software product needs development, hosting, security, support and maintenance.

Partners, suppliers and distribution channels can be part of the delivery model. If an organization depends on a third party for a critical activity, that dependency belongs in the analysis.

Understand how the organization captures value

Revenue may come from one-time sales, subscriptions, usage fees, commissions, licensing or service contracts. Some organizations are funded by grants, donations or public budgets rather than customer purchases. The model should identify who pays, when payment occurs and what costs must be covered.

A business can have strong customer interest and still struggle if the price does not support the cost of delivery. It can also generate accounting profit but face cash-flow pressure when customers pay late. Basic financial planning helps test whether the model is sustainable.

An original four-question model review

Use this short worksheet to think through an idea. It is a learning exercise created for this lesson, not a reproduction of a commercial canvas or proprietary template.

  1. Need: who has the problem, and what evidence shows it matters?
  2. Offer: what useful change or outcome does the organization provide?
  3. Delivery: what people, activities, tools and partners are required to provide it reliably?
  4. Support: who pays or funds the work, what does delivery cost and when does money arrive?

Worked example: a campus repair service

Imagine a student-run service that helps classmates arrange laptop repairs. The need is access to reliable diagnosis and clear repair status. The offer is a booking and coordination service. Delivery requires vetted technicians, a booking form, customer communication and a method for recording device handovers. The service may earn a coordination fee or a disclosed margin on repair work.

The students should test the idea with potential users, verify whether technicians can meet the expected standard, understand responsibility for devices and calculate whether the fee covers the coordination effort. The example is a planning exercise, not a recommendation to handle customer devices without appropriate safeguards.

Practice task

  1. Choose a local business, online service or student project.
  2. Answer the four questions in the worksheet.
  3. List one assumption that needs customer research.
  4. Identify one delivery cost and one possible source of income.
  5. Suggest one small test that could show whether the idea is useful.

Quick review

  • A business model explains how value is created, delivered and captured.
  • A product idea is not enough; the customer problem must be understood.
  • Delivery requires resources, activities and sometimes partners.
  • Revenue, funding, costs and timing affect sustainability.
  • Small tests can reveal whether assumptions are realistic.
