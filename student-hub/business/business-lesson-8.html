Break-Even Analysis for Beginners: Formula, Example and Practice

Break-even analysis helps a business estimate how many units it must sell before revenue covers its costs. This lesson explains fixed costs, variable costs and contribution margin, then uses a simple example so students can calculate and interpret a break-even point.

Prepared by Zia CarterNovember 18, 2026Beginner lesson · 35-minute lesson
Back to Business lessons

What you will learn

  • Distinguish fixed and variable costs.
  • Calculate contribution per unit and contribution margin ratio.
  • Calculate break-even units and revenue.
  • Interpret the assumptions behind a break-even result.
  • Use break-even analysis in a simple business decision.

What is break-even analysis?

Break-even analysis estimates the sales volume at which total revenue equals total costs. At that point, the business makes neither an operating profit nor an operating loss under the assumptions used. Sales above the point may create profit; sales below it may create a loss.

It is a planning tool, not a guarantee. The result depends on the selling price, variable cost per unit, fixed costs, product mix and the time period being measured.

Understand fixed costs and variable costs

Cost typeMeaningSimple example
Fixed costDoes not change with the number of units over the selected range and period.Monthly rent or a fixed software subscription.
Variable costChanges with the number of units or services delivered.Materials or packaging needed for each unit.
Mixed or semi-variable costContains a fixed part and a usage-related part.A service fee with an allowance plus extra usage charges.

Some costs behave differently when a business grows beyond its current capacity. Rent may rise when a larger space is needed; labour may increase in steps when another shift is added. The model should state the relevant time period and operating range.

What is contribution margin?

Contribution margin per unit is the selling price minus the variable cost of producing or delivering one unit. It is the amount from each sale available to cover fixed costs first. After fixed costs are covered, additional contribution can contribute to operating profit.

Contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit.

The contribution margin ratio expresses that contribution as a share of selling price:

Contribution margin ratio = contribution per unit ÷ selling price.

Use the break-even formula

Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.

For an estimate in sales revenue:

Break-even revenue = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin ratio.

The formula assumes a stable selling price, a stable variable cost per unit and a meaningful estimate of fixed costs for the period. If there are several products with different margins, a single-product calculation may be misleading unless a realistic sales mix is used.

Worked example: a small campus print service

Imagine a fictional student-run print service with monthly fixed costs of $1,800. It charges $30 per print bundle, and paper, ink and packaging cost $12 for each bundle.

StepCalculationResult
Contribution per bundle$30 − $12$18
Contribution margin ratio$18 ÷ $3060%
Break-even units$1,800 ÷ $18100 bundles
Break-even revenue$1,800 ÷ 0.60$3,000

Under these assumptions, the service must sell 100 bundles in the month to cover the $1,800 fixed cost plus the variable costs attached to those 100 bundles. The example uses round figures for learning; an actual business should use its own records and currency.

How to interpret the result

  • If expected sales are below 100 bundles, the plan may not cover the estimated monthly costs.
  • If expected sales are close to 100, small changes in price, costs or demand may affect the result.
  • If expected sales are above 100, the model estimates a contribution beyond the fixed-cost break-even point.

For example, at 120 bundles, contribution is 120 × $18 = $2,160. After the $1,800 fixed cost, the simplified operating result is $360 before any costs omitted from the model, taxes, financing or other adjustments.

Test what happens when assumptions change

Break-even analysis is especially useful when comparing scenarios. Ask what happens if material costs rise, the price changes, rent increases or a promotion reduces the selling price. Recalculate contribution per unit before trusting the old break-even number.

ChangeLikely effect if other inputs stay equal
Higher selling priceRaises contribution per unit and usually lowers break-even units.
Higher variable costReduces contribution per unit and usually raises break-even units.
Higher fixed costRaises the number of units needed to cover costs.
Lower selling priceReduces contribution unless variable costs also fall.

Common break-even mistakes

  • Leaving out costs because they are paid quarterly or annually; convert them to the period being analyzed.
  • Calling every monthly expense fixed even when part changes with usage.
  • Using revenue instead of contribution per unit in the unit formula.
  • Ignoring discounts, returns, payment fees or sales commissions where they are material.
  • Using one product's margin for a business that sells several products with different sales mixes.
  • Treating a forecast as a guarantee rather than a scenario based on assumptions.

Practice task

A school club sells custom notebooks for $18 each. The variable cost per notebook is $7 and monthly fixed costs are $550.

  1. Calculate contribution per notebook.
  2. Calculate the break-even number of notebooks.
  3. Calculate the contribution margin ratio.
  4. Estimate break-even revenue.
  5. Explain what might change if the notebook price falls to $16.

Check your calculation: contribution is $11 per notebook; break-even units are $550 ÷ $11 = 50 notebooks; the contribution margin ratio is about 61.1%; estimated break-even revenue is $900.

Quick review

  • Fixed costs are stable over a selected range and period.
  • Variable costs change with units or service volume.
  • Contribution per unit is price minus variable cost per unit.
  • Break-even units are fixed costs divided by contribution per unit.
  • The result is only as reliable as its assumptions.

Frequently asked questions

What is the break-even point in simple words?

The break-even point is the sales level where total revenue equals total costs for the period being analyzed. At that level, the business has covered its modeled costs but has not yet generated an operating profit or loss under those assumptions.

What is the formula for break-even units?

Divide total fixed costs for the chosen period by contribution margin per unit. Contribution per unit equals the selling price minus variable cost per unit. The formula assumes the price and variable cost remain reasonably stable across the sales range being examined.

Why do businesses calculate contribution margin?

Contribution margin shows how much each sale contributes toward fixed costs after variable costs are covered. It helps a business estimate break-even volume, compare pricing and cost scenarios, and understand why higher sales do not always produce higher profit when demand or costs change.

Can a business have more than one break-even point?

Yes. A business may have different break-even estimates for different products, service lines, price levels or operating ranges. It may also calculate separate points for different periods. With multiple products, the result depends on the assumed sales mix and each product's contribution.

What are the limitations of break-even analysis?

It simplifies reality by assuming stable prices, costs and sales mix over a chosen range. It may omit capacity constraints, changing demand, discounts, financing costs, taxes or unexpected expenses. Use it as a planning estimate and revisit the inputs when business conditions change.

Further learning

After calculating a break-even point, compare the result with your revenue forecast, cash timing and one-page business plan. A business can break even on paper and still face cash-flow pressure if customers pay late or inventory must be funded first.
