How to Write a One-Page Business Plan: A Beginner's Guide

A useful business plan does not have to begin as a long report. A one-page plan helps you organize the customer problem, your offer, how the business will reach buyers, how it will earn revenue and what evidence you still need to collect.

What you will learn

  • Explain what a one-page business plan is used for.

What is a one-page business plan?

A one-page business plan is a concise summary of a business idea, its customers, value proposition, delivery approach, revenue, costs and next milestones. It is useful for clarifying an early idea and guiding small tests, but it may not replace a detailed plan required by a lender or investor.

Start with the customer problem

Describe a specific group of people and a problem they experience. Avoid broad statements such as “everyone needs better service.” A useful problem statement explains who has the problem, when it occurs and why current alternatives are not good enough.

Try this sentence: “For [customer group], [problem] makes it difficult to [desired task]. Current alternatives are [option], but they do not solve [specific gap].” Treat this as a hypothesis until you have spoken with potential customers or observed real demand.

Write the value proposition and offer

Explain what you provide and why it helps the chosen customer. A value proposition should connect a customer need to a practical benefit. It is not a list of every feature you hope to build.

Separate the smallest useful offer from future additions. A student business idea might begin with one service, one customer group and one delivery method. Keeping the first version narrow makes it easier to test whether customers actually want it.

Map the customer and alternatives

Identify the first customer group you plan to serve. Describe relevant needs, location or work context, buying triggers and who makes the decision. Then list direct competitors and indirect alternatives, including doing nothing or completing the task manually.

Do not claim that there are no competitors. If customers already solve the problem another way, that alternative competes for their time and money even if it is not the same product.

Explain how the business reaches and serves customers

Choose a small number of channels that fit the customer. These might include referrals, a local partnership, a website, a marketplace, a direct sales conversation or a professional network. State how a person moves from discovering the offer to asking a question, purchasing and receiving the service.

Also identify the resources and activities needed to deliver the offer: equipment, skills, suppliers, software, time, permits or support. A plan is more useful when it shows the work behind the promise.

Describe revenue, prices and major costs

Explain what the customer pays for and how often. A business may earn revenue from one-time sales, recurring subscriptions, service packages, commissions or project fees. State the pricing assumption and what it includes.

List the main costs rather than trying to predict every future expense. Consider materials, software, transport, payment processing, marketing, labor, rent and support. Revenue is not profit, and a profitable sale can still create a cash-flow problem if the customer pays later than the supplier requires payment.

Set measurable milestones and identify assumptions

Turn the business idea into a short sequence of tests. A milestone should describe an outcome, an owner and a date—not just an activity such as “work on marketing.” Examples include interviewing potential customers, delivering a pilot, confirming a supplier or tracking whether customers return.

Mark uncertain claims as assumptions. If you do not know the likely price, customer demand or delivery cost, write down how you will test it instead of presenting a guess as a fact.

Worked example: a fictional campus bicycle repair service

Imagine a student considering a small bicycle repair service near a campus. The first customer group is students who use bicycles for daily travel. The problem hypothesis is that minor repairs are inconvenient between classes. The offer is a limited set of routine repairs with booking by message and a clear price list.

Possible alternatives include a nearby repair shop, repairing the bicycle personally or delaying the repair. Revenue might come from a fee for each completed job. Costs could include tools, replacement parts, transport and time. The first milestone is not to buy a large inventory; it is to interview potential users and test whether they would book a small pilot service.

This is a fictional teaching example, not evidence of market demand or a real business forecast. Replace every assumption with local research, actual supplier quotes and customer feedback.

When one page is not enough

A concise plan is useful for internal clarity and early learning. A bank, investor, grant provider or regulated activity may require detailed financial projections, market research, ownership information, risk analysis or supporting documents. Check the recipient's requirements before relying on a one-page summary alone.

Practice task

  1. Choose one business idea and one specific customer group.
  2. Write the problem in one sentence.
  3. Describe the offer and its customer benefit.
  4. List two alternatives customers use now.
  5. Write one revenue assumption and three major cost categories.
  6. Choose one low-cost test that could disprove your main assumption.

Quick review

  • A one-page plan summarizes the decisions behind a business idea.
  • Customer, problem and value should be specific.
  • Revenue and cost assumptions should be visible.
  • Milestones should describe measurable outcomes.
  • Early assumptions need evidence from customer conversations and tests.

Frequently asked questions

What should be included in a one-page business plan?

Include the business idea, target customer, problem, value proposition, alternatives, offer, channels, revenue model, major costs, resources, milestones and key assumptions. Keep each section brief and specific. The page should help a reader understand the business logic and identify what still needs evidence.

Can a business plan really fit on one page?

An early or lean plan can fit on one page by summarizing the main decisions rather than explaining every detail. It is a working overview, not a complete substitute for detailed financial forecasts, market research or documents requested by a bank, investor or regulator.

How do I validate a business plan?

Test its riskiest assumptions with potential customers, small pilots, supplier quotes or a simple landing page. Record what people actually do, not only what they say. Use the evidence to revise the customer, offer, price or delivery method before committing significant resources.

What is the difference between a lean plan and a traditional business plan?

A lean plan summarizes the core business model and the assumptions that need testing. A traditional plan provides more detail about the market, operations, management, financial projections and funding. Choose the format that fits the business stage and the requirements of the person who will read it.

How often should a one-page business plan be reviewed?

Review it whenever important evidence changes, such as customer demand, costs, pricing, supplier availability or delivery capacity. A regular monthly or milestone-based review can help, but the schedule should fit the speed of the business. Update assumptions when new evidence replaces old estimates.
