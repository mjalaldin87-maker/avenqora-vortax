Cash Flow Forecasting for Small Businesses: A Beginner's Guide

A cash flow forecast estimates when money is expected to enter and leave a business. It helps owners plan for quiet periods, late customer payments and large bills. This beginner lesson explains the basic structure, a worked monthly example and ways to improve forecast assumptions.

Cash flow forecasting for small businesses means estimating the cash a business expects to receive and pay over a future period. It is not the same as predicting profit. A business can record sales and still face a cash shortage if customers pay later than bills fall due.

Cash flow versus profit

Profit compares income earned with expenses recognised during an accounting period. Cash flow focuses on the timing of money entering and leaving the bank account. An invoice may count as a sale before the customer pays it, so the cash forecast should use the expected payment date.

The basic forecast structure

LineMeaning
Opening cashMoney available at the start of the period
Cash receiptsCustomer payments and other money expected to arrive
Total cash availableOpening cash plus receipts
Cash paymentsSuppliers, wages, rent, utilities, tax and other payments
Net cash movementReceipts minus payments
Closing cashOpening cash plus net cash movement

For a simple monthly forecast, use this relationship:

Closing cash = Opening cash + Cash received − Cash paid

The closing cash for one month becomes the opening cash for the next month. A negative closing balance signals a possible shortfall that needs attention before the payment date arrives.

Build the forecast step by step

  1. Choose the planning period. A short-term weekly view may help manage near-term obligations; a monthly view can help identify a longer seasonal pattern. Choose a period that matches the business's cash cycle.
  2. Enter the opening balance. Start with the real bank balance at the beginning of the forecast period, adjusted for payments that have already been initiated but not cleared.
  3. Estimate customer receipts. Use realistic expected payment dates rather than invoice dates. Separate confirmed payments from uncertain sales estimates.
  4. List every major payment. Include suppliers, rent, wages, subscriptions, loan payments, tax, insurance, marketing and irregular costs.
  5. Calculate net movement and closing cash. Use formulas so each month's closing balance rolls forward.
  6. Compare forecast with actual results. Update assumptions when customer behaviour, costs or trading conditions change.

Worked example: a small design studio

Imagine a studio begins April with $4,000 in its business account. It expects $7,500 in customer receipts during April and plans to pay $6,200 in operating costs. This simplified example excludes tax-specific treatment and is only used to demonstrate the arithmetic.

April forecastAmount
Opening cash$4,000
Expected receipts$7,500
Cash available$11,500
Expected payments$6,200
Net cash movement$1,300
Closing cash$5,300

If a customer delays a $3,000 payment until May, April receipts fall to $4,500. With the same opening balance and planned payments, the closing cash becomes $2,300. The studio may still be able to pay its bills, but it has less room for unexpected costs. That difference is why payment timing matters.

Make assumptions visible

Record why each estimate was chosen. Use past receipts when available, current contracts, supplier quotes and a cautious estimate for new sales. Note seasonal changes, annual subscriptions and bills that arrive quarterly rather than dividing every cost evenly across twelve months.

  • Use the customer's normal payment behaviour, not just the invoice due date.
  • Keep one-off purchases separate from regular monthly costs.
  • Include taxes and loan payments in the periods when they are expected to be paid.
  • Separate confirmed receipts from uncertain opportunities.
  • Prepare a lower-income or delayed-payment scenario for important decisions.

Spreadsheet layout and formula checks

A basic worksheet can use one column per week or month and one row per cash category. Create formulas for total receipts, total payments, net movement and closing cash. Link each closing balance to the following period's opening balance, and label assumptions so another person can understand the forecast.

Check that totals match the underlying rows, dates are in the right period and the first opening balance matches the bank records. Do not confuse a transfer between two of the business's own accounts with new revenue. Keep the forecast separate from personal spending unless the business plan explicitly includes owner drawings.

Review and act on the forecast

Update the forecast on a regular schedule and whenever a major customer payment, supplier cost or business decision changes. Compare actual cash received and paid with the estimate. For a predicted shortfall, speak with customers or suppliers early, review discretionary spending and consider suitable funding with qualified advice where needed.

A forecast is a planning tool, not a guarantee. It does not replace accounting records, tax advice or a full financial plan. Business owners should seek professional advice for complex financing, tax, insolvency or legal decisions.

Frequently asked questions

What is a cash flow forecast?

A cash flow forecast estimates the money a business expects to receive and pay over a future period. It carries the opening cash balance forward after planned receipts and payments so owners can see likely surpluses, possible shortfalls and the timing of important financial commitments.

Is cash flow the same as profit?

No. Profit is calculated using accounting income and expenses, while cash flow tracks when money actually enters or leaves the business. A sale on credit may contribute to reported revenue before the customer pays, which can create a gap between profit and available cash.

How far ahead should a small business forecast?

Choose a period long enough to cover the business's cash cycle and important commitments. Some owners use weekly forecasts for short-term control and monthly forecasts for planning. A longer forecast can be useful, but estimates further into the future usually need more frequent review.

What should be included in a cash flow forecast?

Include opening cash, expected customer receipts, other income, supplier costs, wages, rent, utilities, tax, loan payments and irregular expenses. Focus on when cash is expected to move, not simply when a sale is recorded or an invoice is issued. Use the actual expected payment date.

How often should a cash flow forecast be updated?

Review it regularly and whenever a material assumption changes, such as a delayed customer payment, new supplier cost or unexpected bill. Compare forecast amounts with actual bank movements, update the remaining periods and record why important assumptions have changed. Assign one person to maintain the update.

Key takeaway

A cash flow forecast helps a business see whether cash is likely to be available when payments are due. Start with a real opening balance, estimate receipts by payment date, list expected outgoings and roll the closing balance forward. Review actual results and update assumptions as circumstances change.
