Inventory Management for Small Businesses: Stock, Reorder Points and Counts

Inventory management for small businesses means knowing what stock is available, what has been sold or used, what is on order and when to replenish it. A simple, consistent record can help a small shop avoid preventable stockouts, reduce over-ordering and understand where money is tied up in products.

What is inventory management?

Inventory management is the process of recording, storing, tracking and replenishing the goods or materials a business needs. It covers items purchased for resale, raw materials, work in progress and finished products. The right system depends on the range of items and how quickly stock moves.

Inventory is not just a count of boxes. A useful record connects quantities with product identity, location, supplier, cost and movement. When a sale, return, damage or delivery occurs, the record should be updated promptly.

Which inventory records should a small business keep?

Start with a product code, description, unit of measure, quantity on hand, supplier, purchase cost, selling price and storage location. Record each receipt, sale, return, damage and adjustment so expected stock can be compared with what is physically present.

A spreadsheet can be enough for a small range if one person owns updates and every transaction is recorded. A point-of-sale or inventory system may make more sense when sales are frequent, there are several locations or multiple people change stock records.

What is a reorder point?

A reorder point is the stock level at which a business should place a replenishment order. A simple starting formula is average demand during supplier lead time plus safety stock. It helps owners order before normal demand consumes the remaining supply.

Reorder point = expected demand during lead time + safety stock.

For example, if a shop sells 8 units per day and delivery normally takes 5 days, expected demand during lead time is 40 units. If the owner adds 10 units as a planning buffer, the reorder point is 50 units. This is an illustrative rule, not a universal stock policy.

What is safety stock?

Safety stock is extra inventory held to reduce the risk of running out when demand is higher than expected or a supplier takes longer to deliver. The amount should reflect demand variation, delivery reliability, storage cost and how serious a stockout would be.

Too little buffer may lead to missed sales; too much can tie up cash and create expiry or obsolescence risk. Review safety stock when sales patterns, supplier lead times or seasonal conditions change.

How can a small business count stock accurately?

Compare the recorded quantity with a physical count, investigate differences and correct the record only after the reason is understood. A full stocktake checks the whole inventory; cycle counting checks selected items regularly so errors can be noticed sooner.

  1. Pause or clearly record stock movements during the count.
  2. Count by item code and unit, not only by product name.
  3. Separate damaged, returned or expired goods from sellable stock.
  4. Recount material differences and check recent receipts and sales.
  5. Record the reason for the adjustment and who approved it.

For a small range, a weekly count of fast-moving or high-value items can complement a broader monthly or quarterly count. The right frequency depends on the business and any accounting, tax or regulatory requirements that apply.

How should a business manage slow-moving or perishable stock?

Review stock age and recent sales to identify items that may expire, become obsolete or occupy valuable space. Apply first-in, first-out for stock that should be sold in the order it arrived, and first-expiry, first-out where the nearest expiry date should take priority.

Stock typeUseful control
Food, cosmetics or medicinesTrack batch and expiry dates; rotate by earliest expiry.
Fashion or seasonal goodsReview age and seasonal demand before reordering.
ElectronicsTrack serial numbers and warranty status where needed.
General fast-moving itemsUse reorder points and regular counts.

What is a simple daily inventory routine?

Use a short routine to record incoming deliveries, sales, returns and damaged goods, then review items that are near their reorder point. A named owner and consistent timing are more useful than a complicated spreadsheet that nobody updates.

  • Check deliveries against the order before adding them to available stock.
  • Record sales and returns on the same day.
  • Review the low-stock list for important items.
  • Separate damaged or expired goods and record the reason.
  • Keep purchase evidence and supplier confirmations together.

Common inventory management mistakes

  • Using different names or units for the same item.
  • Waiting until the shelf is empty before ordering.
  • Ignoring supplier lead-time changes and seasonal demand.
  • Counting stock but failing to investigate differences.
  • Reordering slow-moving products without checking existing stock.
  • Mixing damaged, returned or expired goods with sellable inventory.

Practice task: calculate a reorder point

A stationery shop sells 12 notebooks each day. Its supplier normally delivers in 4 days, and the owner chooses a buffer of 15 notebooks. Calculate demand during lead time and the reorder point. Demand during lead time is 12 × 4 = 48; the reorder point is 48 + 15 = 63 notebooks.

Now change one assumption: if the supplier's delivery time increases to 6 days, demand during lead time becomes 72 and the same buffer produces a reorder point of 87. This shows why reorder levels should be reviewed when supplier reliability changes.

Frequently asked questions

Can a small business manage inventory in a spreadsheet?

Yes, when the product range and transaction volume are manageable and someone updates every receipt, sale, return and adjustment. Use consistent item codes, units and stock locations. Move to dedicated software when manual records become unreliable or difficult to share.

How do you calculate a reorder point?

Estimate average demand per day, multiply it by the supplier's delivery lead time in days, then add safety stock. The result is a planning trigger, not a guaranteed service level. Review it when demand, lead time or business priorities change.

What is the difference between a stocktake and cycle counting?

A stocktake is a physical count of inventory, often covering the full range. Cycle counting checks selected items on a repeating schedule. Cycle counting can detect discrepancies sooner, while periodic full counts may still be needed for financial or operational reasons.

What does FIFO mean in inventory management?

FIFO means first in, first out: the earliest received stock is issued or sold first. It is useful where older stock should move before newer stock. For goods with expiry dates, first-expiry, first-out may be a better rotation rule.

How can a business reduce overstocking?

Review recent sales, supplier lead times, minimum order quantities and seasonal changes before buying. Identify slow-moving items and avoid automatic reorders that ignore current stock. Use reorder points as a guide and review unusual purchases before placing an order.

Key takeaway

Small-business inventory control starts with accurate item records and a consistent routine. Track every movement, calculate reorder points using lead time and demand, count stock regularly and review slow-moving or perishable goods before buying more.
