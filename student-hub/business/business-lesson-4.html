Marketing Fundamentals: Customers, Positioning and the Marketing Mix

Marketing helps an organization understand people it wants to serve and explain why its product or service may be useful to them. This lesson introduces customer needs, market research, positioning and the marketing mix.

What you will learn

  • Distinguish customer need, product and value proposition
  • Explain basic market research and positioning
  • Describe the four elements of the marketing mix

What is marketing?

Marketing is the work of understanding a market, designing an offer for a group of customers and communicating how that offer can meet a need. It is broader than advertising. Research, product decisions, pricing, distribution and customer feedback all influence whether an offer reaches the right people.

Start with the customer problem

A useful marketing plan begins with a clear question: whose problem are we trying to solve? Avoid describing the audience only as “everyone.” A student, a small business owner and a procurement manager may need different information and have different reasons to choose a product.

Identify the customer's situation, the task they want to complete, the alternatives they already use and the barriers that could prevent a purchase. Interviews, surveys, public reports, search behavior and competitor research can provide evidence. Treat assumptions as questions to test rather than facts.

Segmentation and positioning

Segmentation means grouping a market into meaningful sets of customers who share relevant characteristics or needs. A business may segment by organization size, industry, usage pattern, location, needs or buying behavior.

Positioning describes how an organization wants its offer to be understood in relation to alternatives. A clear position connects the customer problem, the offer's meaningful difference and the evidence that supports the claim. Avoid vague promises such as “best quality” unless the business can explain what that means and demonstrate it.

The marketing mix: four connected decisions

  • Product: what the organization offers, including features, service, support and the experience around it.
  • Price: what the customer pays and how the price relates to value, costs, alternatives and payment terms.
  • Place: how the offer reaches the customer, such as a website, shop, distributor, marketplace or direct sales channel.
  • Promotion: how the organization communicates with the market through useful content, advertising, events, public relations or sales conversations.

The four decisions affect one another. A premium service may require a delivery and support model that justifies its price. A low-cost digital product may depend on self-service onboarding and online distribution. Changing one decision can create a need to review the others.

Choose communication that helps a decision

Promotion should explain the offer clearly and help the intended customer evaluate it. A useful product page may answer who the offer is for, what problem it solves, how it works, what it costs and where a buyer can get support. A technical buyer may need specifications, while a first-time customer may need a simple demonstration.

Measure more than clicks

Marketing measures should connect to the business objective. Depending on the activity, a team may review qualified inquiries, product trials, conversion between stages, repeat purchases, customer feedback or contribution after campaign costs. A high number of visits is not necessarily useful if visitors are not the intended audience or cannot complete the next step.

Worked example: a tutoring service

Imagine a tutoring service for first-year computing students. Research suggests that students struggle most with introductory programming practice. The service can position itself around structured beginner exercises and feedback, offer a weekly group plan, price it transparently, distribute it through a website and student societies, and promote a free sample lesson. Each choice supports the same customer need.

Practice task

  1. Choose a product or service you understand.
  2. Describe one customer group and the problem it wants to solve.
  3. Write one sentence explaining how the offer is positioned.
  4. Describe one decision for product, price, place and promotion.
  5. Choose one measure that would show whether the marketing activity is working.

Quick review

  • Marketing begins with understanding customers and their needs.
  • Segmentation groups customers by meaningful differences.
  • Positioning explains how an offer is intended to be understood.
  • Product, price, place and promotion are connected decisions.
  • Useful measures connect activity to a business objective.
