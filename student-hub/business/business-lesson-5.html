Business Finance Basics: Revenue, Expenses, Profit and Cash Flow

Business finance helps an organization understand what it earns, what it spends and whether it has enough cash to meet its obligations. This lesson explains a few essential terms using a simple example.

What you will learn

  • Distinguish revenue, expenses and profit
  • Explain why profit and cash flow are different
  • Read a basic financial example and calculate a margin

Why basic finance matters

Every organization makes decisions about prices, purchases, wages, rent, equipment and future plans. Basic financial information helps owners and managers see whether the business is earning enough, using resources responsibly and able to pay its obligations when they fall due.

Revenue is money earned from sales

Revenue is the income a business earns from providing goods or services during a period. It is usually measured before subtracting the costs of running the business. A shop may earn revenue from product sales; a consulting service may earn revenue from client fees.

Expenses are the costs of operating

Expenses are costs recognized in running the business, such as wages, rent, utilities, software subscriptions, advertising or supplies. Some costs change with activity, while others are relatively fixed over a period. The exact accounting treatment depends on the type of transaction and the accounting rules being used.

Profit is not the same as revenue

A simplified income calculation is:

Profit = Revenue - Expenses

Illustrative example: A small service business records $1,500 in revenue for a month and $1,100 in expenses. Its simplified profit is $400. The business has not earned $1,500 of profit; it has to account for the costs required to generate the revenue.

A profit margin expresses profit as a share of revenue:

Profit margin = (Profit / Revenue) × 100

Using the example, the margin is approximately 26.7%. This is a teaching calculation, not a target margin for every business.

Cash flow answers a different question

Cash flow tracks money actually entering and leaving the business. Profit and cash movement can occur at different times. A business may complete work and record revenue before a customer pays the invoice. It may show a profit on paper while waiting for cash that is needed to pay suppliers or staff.

Cash can also leave the business to buy equipment or repay a loan. Those transactions may not appear as ordinary operating expenses in the same way that rent or utilities do. This is one reason financial statements provide different views of the business.

Three common financial statements

  • Income statement: summarizes revenue and expenses over a period and reports profit or loss.
  • Balance sheet: shows assets, liabilities and equity at a particular date.
  • Cash flow statement: explains cash received and cash paid across operating, investing and financing activities.

Each statement answers a different question. They should be read together rather than treated as interchangeable reports.

Plan for bills and timing

A simple cash plan lists when customer payments are expected and when important bills must be paid. It can show a temporary shortage even when sales are growing. Managers may then need to improve invoice follow-up, adjust payment terms, delay a non-essential purchase or arrange suitable funding.

Worked example: an invoice paid later

A designer completes a $600 project in March and sends an invoice. The work may be recorded as revenue in March under an accrual accounting approach, but the customer may pay in April. The March income statement and the March bank balance therefore tell different stories. A cash plan helps the designer prepare for expenses due before the invoice is paid.

Practice task

  1. A business earns $2,400 in sales and has $1,750 in expenses. Calculate the simplified profit.
  2. Calculate the profit margin using the formula above.
  3. Give one reason a profitable business may still have a cash shortage.
  4. Name the financial statement that shows assets and liabilities at a date.

Quick review

  • Revenue is earned from sales or services.
  • Expenses are costs recognized in operating the business.
  • Profit is revenue minus expenses in a simplified calculation.
  • Cash flow tracks when money actually enters and leaves.
  • Financial statements provide different but connected views.
