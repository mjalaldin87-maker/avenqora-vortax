Professional Email and Online Communication

Professional online communication is clear, respectful and appropriate for the audience. This lesson explains how to structure an email, use recipient fields correctly and avoid common communication and privacy mistakes.

Prepared by Zia CarterNovember 5, 2026Beginner lesson
Back to Digital Skills lessons

What you will learn

  • Structure an email with a useful subject, greeting, purpose and action
  • Choose To, CC and BCC recipients carefully
  • Communicate professionally while protecting sensitive information

Why professional email still matters

Email is used for university work, job applications, project coordination, customer communication and formal requests. A well-written message helps the recipient understand why you are writing and what response or action is needed.

Professional communication does not need complicated vocabulary. Clear wording, correct names, useful context and a respectful tone usually matter more than sounding formal for its own sake.

Use a subject line that describes the purpose

A useful subject line helps the recipient recognize the topic before opening the message. Include a course, project, reference or action when it helps distinguish the email. Avoid vague subjects such as “Hello” or “Important” when you can provide more context.

Choose recipients deliberately

  • To: people expected to act or respond.
  • CC: people who need visibility but are not the main action owner.
  • BCC: recipients whose addresses should not be visible to other recipients, when there is a legitimate reason to use it.

Do not add people simply because they are senior or might be interested. Check the recipient list before sending, especially when forwarding or replying to a long thread.

Build a clear message structure

  1. Start with an appropriate greeting.
  2. Give a short sentence explaining who you are if the recipient may not recognize you.
  3. State the reason for writing near the beginning.
  4. Provide only the context needed to understand the request.
  5. Make the requested action, deadline or question easy to find.
  6. Close politely and include a useful signature.

Make requests easy to answer

Instead of writing “Please help me with the project,” explain what you need. For example: “Could you confirm whether the revised outline is suitable for Friday's submission?” A focused question makes it easier for the recipient to respond.

If a message contains several issues, separate them into short paragraphs or a numbered list. Keep long background explanations in an attachment or document only when they are genuinely necessary.

Tone and timing

Use standard spelling, punctuation and capitalization. Avoid writing an entire email in capital letters or using chat abbreviations in a formal message. Match the greeting and level of detail to the relationship. A professor, employer, supplier and close teammate may expect different forms of address.

Before sending an urgent message, consider whether the deadline is reasonable and whether the recipient has the information needed to act. Follow up politely if a response is overdue, rather than sending repeated messages without new context.

Attachments, links and privacy

Check that the file is the correct version and that its name is understandable. Do not send passwords, payment credentials or other secrets by ordinary email. Use approved secure sharing methods for sensitive documents. Check link access before sending a cloud document and make sure the recipient has permission to view it.

Remember that email can be forwarded. Avoid including confidential details that the recipient does not need. If you receive an unexpected request to change payment details or provide account information, verify it through a separate trusted channel.

Worked example: asking for a meeting

A student wants to ask a lecturer for a short meeting about a research proposal. A clear email would identify the course, explain the proposal question in one or two sentences, offer two possible meeting times and attach the outline only if requested or useful. The subject should name the course and meeting purpose.

Practice task

  1. Write a subject line for a request to review a project outline.
  2. Identify who should be in To and who, if anyone, should be in CC.
  3. Rewrite “I need help urgently” as a specific, polite request.
  4. List two checks to make before sharing an attachment.

Quick review

  • Use a meaningful subject line.
  • Keep the purpose and requested action near the beginning.
  • Choose recipients based on their role in the conversation.
  • Use a courteous tone and proofread before sending.
  • Do not send passwords or unnecessary confidential information.
